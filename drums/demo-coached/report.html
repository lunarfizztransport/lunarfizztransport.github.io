What the research says

Published studies on timing and groove that put your numbers in context. Every source is listed at the bottom of this page.

How small a difference can anyone hear?

At this tempo your 16th notes are 188 ms apart and 8th notes 375 ms apart. Research puts the smallest noticeable timing change at about 6 ms for 16ths and 9 ms for 8ths at this tempo. Differences smaller than that are below what listeners can reliably hear. [1]

What does tight look like?

Your spread is 24 ms. For a professional benchmark, Jeff Porcaro's famous hi-hat part varied by about 9 ms between hits. (That's a slightly different measure from this tool's spread, so treat it as a ballpark, not an exact target.) Research also finds trained musicians are less variable than beginners, so this number should come down with practice. The 'typical' (10–20 ms) and 'loose' (20–30 ms) spread labels are this tool's rules of thumb, not research findings. [2][3]

Why am I ahead of the click?

You were 18 ms ahead of the click on average. Landing early is one of the most consistent findings in timing research: most people play tens of milliseconds ahead of a beat they're following, and trained musicians do it less. It's normal, and it shrinks with practice. [4][3]

Behind the beat can be a choice

Laid-back, on-the-beat and pushed are styles expert drummers play on purpose, for example a snare slightly behind the hi-hat. So a drum that sits late in the 'By drum' chart isn't automatically a mistake. What matters is whether it's deliberate and consistent. [5][6]

Tighter isn't automatically better

Research is split here. Listeners rated a perfectly quantized drum pattern highest, but in another study expert listeners' bodies moved more to music with human timing. Every human performance drifts in small, connected ways, and listeners prefer that over machine-made randomness. Use these numbers to get control of your time, not to sound like a machine. [7][8][9]

Creeping ahead of the click over the take

The tempo change is tiny, but it adds up. By bar 8 you're about 40 ms ahead of the click, which is close to a quarter of a 16th note at this tempo. In a band that plays to a click or backing track, the bass player will hear your hits flamming against theirs. If you're locked to a sequenced part, it will start to sound like two slightly different songs.

Try this: Do the gap-click exercise below. Hearing the click less often forces you to hold the tempo yourself, then shows you right away whether you drifted. Also aim to sit relaxed on the click rather than chase it. Starting slightly behind and ending ahead means you're accelerating into it.

Swing & feel

Your feel is straight: 51.7% on the 8ths and 50.0% on the 16ths, where 50% is perfectly straight. That's right for this kind of syncopated tom groove and for most rock and pop at 80 BPM. The thing shaping your feel isn't swing. It's that your downbeats sit a little late (+12.5 ms) while your 'a' notes rush (-34.7 ms). That squeezes the syncopations together and stretches the beats, which can sound uneven rather than deliberately laid back. A pushed or laid-back feel can be a great choice, but it should be the same every time. Right now it changes as you get busier.

Practice exercises

Gap click start at 70 bpm

Set the click to 80 BPM. Play your main groove for 4 bars with the click on, then mute it for 4 bars and keep going. Many metronome apps have a 'gap' or 'mute bars' setting. When the click comes back, notice whether you're ahead, behind or right on it. Once 4-on/4-off feels solid, move to 2 bars on and 6 off. Record yourself and check that you stay on the click through bars 5-8.

Goal: Come back in on the click (no audible flam) after 4 muted bars, ten times in a row. Then do the same at 80 BPM.

'a' placement ladder start at 60 bpm

Play quiet 16th notes on the hi-hat and count '1 e & a' out loud. Put a snare hit only on the 'a' of 2, then only on the 'a' of 1, then on both. Keep the 'a' snare hits locked to a hi-hat stroke so they fuse into one sound. Then drop the hi-hat 16ths down to 8ths, then to quarters, and keep the 'a' in exactly the same spot. Finally, play your main groove and the 'a of 1' idea from bars 6-8.

Goal: No flam between the snare and the hi-hat on the 'a'. Then keep the 'a' just as late (not rushed) with only quarter-note hi-hats, at 80 BPM.

Feet first, add one hand at a time start at 70 bpm

With the click on, loop just the kick on 1 and the hi-hat on 2, 3 and 4 for one minute. Add the floor tom part for a minute, then the snare, then the high tom. Each time you add a part, listen for it to land with the click and the feet, not in front of them.

Goal: Every layer stays locked to the click for a full minute without your hands pulling ahead.

Variation endurance start at 72 bpm

Play 4 bars of the main groove, then 4 bars of a variation, and repeat for 32 bars total with the click. Record it and listen back to the variation bars specifically: are they rushed compared with the groove bars? Start with the easier variation below and only move on when it's clean.

Goal: The variation bars sound just as relaxed and tight as the groove bars across the full 32-bar recording, at 80 BPM.

Research sources

Each source was checked by hand against the published paper. The coaching and ideas above come from AI and don't cite studies themselves.

  1. Friberg, A., & Sundberg, J. (1995). Time discrimination in a monotonic, isochronous sequence. Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 98(5), 2524–2531. https://doi.org/10.1121/1.413218
    The smallest noticeable timing change is about 6 ms for notes under 240 ms apart, and about 2.5% of the gap between notes for gaps of 240–1000 ms.
  2. Räsänen, E., Pulkkinen, O., Virtanen, T., Zollner, M., & Hennig, H. (2015). Fluctuations of hi-hat timing and dynamics in a virtuoso drum track of a popular music recording. PLOS ONE, 10(6), e0127902. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0127902
    Jeff Porcaro's hi-hat on "I Keep Forgettin'" (96 bpm): the time between hits varied with a standard deviation of about 8.7 ms.
  3. Repp, B. H. (2010). Sensorimotor synchronization and perception of timing: Effects of music training and task experience. Human Movement Science, 29(2), 200–213. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.humov.2009.08.002
    Trained musicians showed smaller asynchronies, lower tapping variability, and greater sensitivity to timing deviations than students with little training.
  4. Repp, B. H. (2005). Sensorimotor synchronization: A review of the tapping literature. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 12(6), 969–992. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03206433
    People playing along to a regular beat usually land tens of milliseconds early (the 'negative mean asynchrony'), one of the most robust findings in timing research.
  5. Danielsen, A., Waadeland, C. H., Sundt, H. G., & Witek, M. A. G. (2015). Effects of instructed timing and tempo on snare drum sound in drum kit performance. Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 138(4), 2301–2316. https://doi.org/10.1121/1.4930950
    Expert drummers asked to play laid-back, on the beat, or pushed could do so deliberately; laid-back snare strokes were also played louder.
  6. Câmara, G. S., Sioros, G., & Danielsen, A. (2022). Mapping timing and intensity strategies in drum-kit performance of a simple back-beat pattern. Journal of New Music Research, 51(1), 3–26. https://doi.org/10.1080/09298215.2022.2150649
    Drummers expressing laid-back or pushed feels used strategies such as playing everything slightly early/late, or one drum early/late against another.
  7. Frühauf, J., Kopiez, R., & Platz, F. (2013). Music on the timing grid: The influence of microtiming on the perceived groove quality of a simple drum pattern performance. Musicae Scientiae, 17(2), 246–260. https://doi.org/10.1177/1029864913486793
    Listeners rated a perfectly quantized rock drum pattern highest; larger timing displacements got lower groove ratings.
  8. Kilchenmann, L., & Senn, O. (2015). Microtiming in swing and funk affects the body movement behavior of music expert listeners. Frontiers in Psychology, 6, 1232. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01232
    Expert listeners moved more to music with human microtiming than to quantized versions, contradicting the idea that exact timing is always best.
  9. Hennig, H., Fleischmann, R., Fredebohm, A., Hagmayer, Y., Nagler, J., Witt, A., Theis, F. J., & Geisel, T. (2011). The nature and perception of fluctuations in human musical rhythms. PLOS ONE, 6(10), e26457. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026457
    Human rhythmic performance always drifts in small, long-range-correlated ways, and listeners prefer that over the random 'humanize' fluctuations in music software.
